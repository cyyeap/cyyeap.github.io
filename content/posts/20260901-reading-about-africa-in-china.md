Before departure for China, I'd set a goal for my month-long China trip: to open my mind and be inspired. While taking the shuttle transport from my transit hotel in Chengdu, I felt extremely grateful that I'm fluent in Mandarin and that I'm able to read literature in Chinese. I certainly never would have thought that I'd find myself on a plane en route from Chengdu to Shanghai, deeply intrigued while reading a chapter in a book about Africa - particularly about countries that I myself had just visited a couple weeks prior. The book was written by a successful Chinese businessman that I didn't know anything about.

It was a random book on the bedside table in my hotel room, just like when Western hotels have bibles. It was written by the hotel's founder who happened to be trip.com's CEO. I'd need to re-read the book but I was thankful that I read it. The book reminded me of a few things: the importance of arts and meditation, curiosity and history. These are the values I value immensely.

My China trip was originally only planned to be one week: it was intended to bring Aidan's parents to visit China. It's a country they wouldn't have visited by themselves, but were delighted to tag along. Then somehow I read about a pet expo happening in Shanghai. Naturally this was of interest to me, so I changed my trip to start earlier. Rather than flying back and forth → over 10 hours each way, I decided to spend about 30 days in China.

Before arriving in China, I hadn't really thought of what to do as I was still recovering from jetlag. I had only returned from Africa a few days earlier. Anyway, before diving too much into my Chinese trip, I decided to write about Africa.

The car from the capital of Kenya — Nairobi — to Maasai Mara Reserve is a 7 hours' long drive. The day before was also the day we visited the Kibaya community: it is the largest slum in the world. We visited two families in the slum, learning how the families live there. My peers and I are very into solving societal problems, so after learning about the families, we jumped straight into problem-solving mode naïvely. We visited two families with many kids. One family, to our shock (not actually logically surprising) had 9 kids (!) sharing a space about the same as a domestic business-class cabin of 6 seats. And there were no toilets in the space they were living in.

One of the many solutions that we came up with to help alleviate poverty was to provide birth control to the women & girls living in the slums. The answer isn't condoms. In slums, rapes and assaults are everyday phenomena, and we'd not expect rapists to have thoughtfully brought condoms along. As a result, the prevalence of AIDS is quite high. Unwanted pregnancy would also be high as a result, in my opinion. Abortion is not common and probably not accessible by the women in the community. So we figured the most appropriate form of birth control would be Implanon. It's probably currently inaccessible to the women in the community, but having a charity to implant & remove Implanon could help reduce unwanted pregnancy. Of course, perception of birth control in the local culture may be negative, so I am not 100% convinced this is the way to go. This topic sparked my interest in pursuing a degree in development policy.

On the day we departed from Kigali to Nairobi, I read that Bill Gates also visited Rwanda while we were there. Honestly, before visiting the place, I wouldn't have been too interested to read about Africa as I felt like it's too far away, and I'd emotionally disconnect myself from engaging with and solving any problems.

Being at the airport in Kigali was a culture shock. I've been to many airports across many countries and I had never had to go through the security logistics that I had to go through in Rwanda and Kenya. That said, the contrast between the checkpoints in Kigali and Nairobi clearly showed the respective competence of the two governments.

Of course, my observations are limited to my own experiences of spending only two days each in these countries. I'm sure Kenya might excel in some parts. For example, the GDP per capita of Kenya is double that of Rwanda.

The best security checkpoint I've witnessed in the world is in Chinese airports - I thought it's genius. Instead of cherry-picking random travellers to perform explosive tests, every single traveller entering the terminals is subject to the explosive tests in bulk. Once the test results return negative, the entire group of travellers is then allowed to enter the departure hall. This thoroughly scans everyone for explosives, with a minimum of overhead. Whereas these are the security checkpoint differences I encountered in Rwanda and Kenya:

In Rwanda, before reaching the airport, there's a pre-airport arrival checkpoint where we had to leave all our luggage in the car, and exit the car. This included the rideshare driver. We had to leave the car for X-ray scanning, while the cars had to go through an explosive detector that looks like a golf club, and with the car & luggage sniffed by trained doggos for any potential explosives. I thought the process was a bit overkill: I had never experienced a pre-airport arrival check before. Perhaps airports in African countries couldn't afford to buy expensive equipment, and that's why they opt for traditional gold detector search.

On the other hand, Rwanda's neighbor Kenya had a pretty hilarious checkpoint procedure. Nairobi has a similar check to Kigali's pre-airport arrival check. Okay, I thought the process would be the same. It's quite odd: the drivers would stay in the car, travellers would have to leave their luggage in the car, and the car wouldn't need to go through any explosive checks. I thought to myself: what if the driver took the car and ran away with my luggage? I wasn't so concerned about my belongings as my passport, which I had stupidly left in the car. Luckily, the driver was benign. I also thought to myself omg, it is so easy to smuggle explosives to Kenya's airport. Just need to be a driver with a passenger. :D

I thought this trip to Africa would be a one-off. That's why we decided to go to both Rwanda and Kenya. Before going there, I thought they would be super duper troublesome countries to visit, as my DNA doesn't allow me to survive well in Africa: Malaria is prevalent and needs preventative treatment, and I also had to be vaccinated against yellow fever. One silver lining is that now I have lifetime immunity. I was pretty sick after the yellow fever vaccination before going there. I did think to myself: is it worth going through this just to see animals, not to mention it's my most expensive trip yet?! I was second guessing my decision. I came to the conclusion after having been there: it's all worth it. There's a Chinese idiom: 读万卷书，不如行万里路 (reading ten thousand books is not as good as travelling ten thousand miles). The essence of it, I've felt so much on this trip. It brought me many new topics of interest: learning about gorillas, Dian Fossey, Rwanda's plastic bans, the Big Five safari animals and much more.

Before going on this trip, Africa was a distant exotic destination. I always thought it's very exclusively for safari, and people who were out of touch. This time we also met up with a fellow Melbournian who lived in Kenya for two years, who chose to move over there and sell vegetables after leaving McKinsey. This was an inspiration. Many of us (probably all my readers), including myself, are living our comfortable lives. This influenced me to reflect on what I really want to do with my life. Now, settling in East Africa may even be a possibility. I never would have thought that I would be obsessed nearly every day with the Big Five. I was super impressed with the development of Rwanda and their conservation efforts. I also learned about the mystery murder of Dian Fossey.
